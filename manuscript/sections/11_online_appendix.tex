\section{Stock market responses to other legal and political events}
\label{sec:ia_other_events}

\noindent The event-study evidence in the main text (Table~\ref{tab:event_study} and Figures~\ref{fig:event1}--\ref{fig:event3}) focuses on the three events that resolved the largest amount of uncertainty about the abrogation: the Joint Resolution, the Supreme Court oral arguments, and the Supreme Court decision. The litigation, however, involved a number of intermediate legal steps. This section examines the stock market response to each of them: the first court hearing on the enforceability of gold clauses (May~22--24, 1933); the first federal ruling upholding the constitutionality of the Joint Resolution (\textit{In re Missouri Pacific R.~Co.}, E.D.~Mo., June~20, 1934); the New York Court of Appeals' affirmance of \textit{Norman v.\ Baltimore \& Ohio R.~Co.} (July~3, 1934); and the Supreme Court's grants of certiorari (October~8 and November~5, 1934). We also examine the November~6, 1934 midterm election.

Table~\ref{tab:other_events} reports, for each event window, the raw market return and the cumulative abnormal returns of the gold-exposed and non-exposed equal-weighted portfolios, using the same portfolio construction, pre-abrogation estimation window, and CAR methodology as Table~\ref{tab:event_study} in the main text. Two results stand out.

First, \textit{none of the intermediate judicial events produced a significant differential response} between gold-exposed and non-exposed firms. The differential is $+0.7$ percentage points around the first hearing, $-0.9$ around \textit{In re Missouri Pacific}, $+0.3$ around the \textit{Norman} affirmance, and $+0.6$ around the first certiorari grant---each small relative to the variability of the daily differential in calm periods. This pattern is consistent with these procedural steps being largely anticipated: both lower-court rulings confirmed the legal status quo (every court to consider the question in 1933--34 upheld the Joint Resolution), so they conveyed little news about the abrogation's ultimate fate. By contrast, the events that carried genuine constitutional and political news---the Joint Resolution itself and the Supreme Court endgame---are precisely where the differential responses appear. We view this as reinforcing the event selection in the main text.

Second, the one exception is the November 1934 window, in which the gold-exposed portfolio earned a cumulative abnormal return 3.2 percentage points above the non-exposed portfolio. This window, however, mixes three tightly interleaved events: the certiorari grant in \textit{United States v.\ Bankers Trust Co.} on Monday, November~5 (which the government itself had sought, accelerating final resolution); the midterm election on Tuesday, November~6, for which the New York Stock Exchange was closed; and the first trading on the election result on Wednesday, November~7. The Democratic landslide---the president's party gained seats in both chambers, an exceptional midterm outcome---was widely read as a popular ratification of the New Deal, and plausibly raised the perceived durability of the abrogation. Figure~\ref{fig:midterm_1934} plots the four portfolio series around this window: the differential opens over November~5--7 and does not subsequently revert, consistent with a genuine repricing rather than a transitory movement. Because the certiorari grant and the election cannot be separated within the window, and because this event was identified in the course of our analysis rather than specified ex ante, we report it here as corroborating evidence rather than as a main result.
