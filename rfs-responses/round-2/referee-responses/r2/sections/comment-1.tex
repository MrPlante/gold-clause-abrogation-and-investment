\noindent Thank you for this suggestion; it changed our exposition of the historical narrative. We first implemented it literally: we built the four requested return series from CRSP daily returns and examined the market's response to every legal development of the litigation, from the Joint Resolution and the first lawsuits through the lower court rulings, the certiorari grants, the oral arguments, and the decision. What we found led us to add an event window to the analysis and to rethink how the paper presents the return evidence.

\medskip
\noindent Regarding the legal events of interest, two findings emerged from this exercise.
\begin{enumerate}
\item An event window the previous draft had missed: November~5--8, 1934. On Monday, November~5, the Supreme Court granted certiorari in \textit{United States v.\ Bankers Trust Co.}, that is, it agreed to review the case. The government itself had petitioned for review, seeking a prompt, definitive ruling on the gold question rather than further lower-court litigation. The very next day, the president's party won an exceptional midterm victory. Both pieces of news bear directly on the uncertainty we study: the certiorari grant on the imminence of its resolution, the election as an indication of popular support for the administration's policies. Because the exchange was closed on election day, we cannot disentangle the effect of the certiorari grant from that of the election result. Over the window, exposed firms outperformed non-exposed firms by 3.5 percentage points ($t = 3.3$). The size split shows that the differential comes from small firms: within the small tercile, exposed firms returned 14.6\% against 6.2\% for non-exposed firms ($+8.4$ percentage points, $t = 3.7$), while the medium and large tercile differentials are positive ($+0.9$ and $+0.1$ percentage points) but not statistically significant.\item None of the lower court events produced a significant differential between exposed and non-exposed firms.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent Below we report what the literal implementation of your suggestions reveals, and then explain how it led to the presentation the paper adopts, which we believe follows the spirit of your suggestion.

\medskip

\noindent Because the events last only a few days each over a nearly two-year period, plotting them all in one graph makes it impossible to visualize the effects. Instead, we plotted individual graphs of the four return series around each event, which we report in Figure~R1 at the end of this document. Generating these figures surfaced two further observations about the four-series representation.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The equal-weighted exposed portfolio and the exposure-weighted portfolio track each other closely in every window, as Figure~R1 shows: weighting exposed firms by the size of their exposure barely changes the portfolio's return. Reporting both series appears redundant and hinders the readability of the figures. Therefore, we keep the equal-weighted version, both because it is the simpler object and because the historical section precedes the formal introduction of the exposure measure, $\tilde{d}$. The figures in the paper (Internet Appendix Figures~IA.2--IA.5) adopt this representation; we reproduce them as Figure~R2 at the end of this document.
\item In the two windows with large positive returns, the Joint Resolution and the certiorari grant and midterm election, the market return sits far below the exposed and non-exposed portfolios alike. The reason is that small firms rallied far more than large firms, raising the question of whether the differential we are documenting only applies to small firms.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The natural way to investigate this question would be to value-weight the two portfolios. In this sample, however, that comparison is dominated by a handful of firms: the five largest of the 177 gold-exposed firms carry $48\%$ of the value-weighted gold portfolio's weight, and the ten largest carry two thirds of the weight. This degree of concentration makes the exercise uncompelling. For that reason, we instead decided to show the results by size group: we sort all firms into terciles on market capitalization at the end of 1932, before the litigation year so that the classification cannot reflect the events themselves, and compare the equal-weighted exposed and non-exposed portfolios within each tercile, alongside the pooled comparison. This is what the paper's Table~1 reports for every event window. In the paper we keep the discussion of why we do not value-weight the portfolios brief (a footnote in Section~3 notes the concentration problem).

\medskip
\noindent Table~1 is the single reference for the return evidence discussion in the main text. It is designed so that every claim in the narrative can be verified in one place: for each main event window, and for the full episode, it reports the cumulative returns of the exposed and non-exposed portfolios and their differential, raw and beta-adjusted, with $t$-statistics scaled by the daily variability of the differential over the pre-abrogation period (July 1926--December 1932). Because each panel also breaks the comparison into size terciles, the reader can check directly whether a differential is broad-based or concentrated among small firms. For convenience, we reproduce Table~1 as Table~R3 at the end of this document.

\medskip
\noindent The central result is the Joint Resolution window (May~26--June~6, 1933): exposed firms returned 30.9\% against 25.4\% for non-exposed firms, a differential of $+5.5$ percentage points ($t = 3.0$), the gold-specific response that the abrogation's debt relief predicts; the differential is positive in every size tercile, strongest among smaller firms and weaker, though still visible, among the largest. The November~5--8, 1934 window is the other significant event, with a differential of $+3.5$ percentage points ($t = 3.3$). The oral-arguments window (January~8--10, 1935) is nearly flat; news of the government's poor showing spread through the press only after the hearings, and a differential of $-1.2$ percentage points ($t = -0.8$) appears over the post-argument days (January~11--17). On the decision day (February~18, 1935), exposed firms returned 3.7\% against 3.2\%, a differential of $+0.5$ percentage points ($t = 0.8$). Section~3 narrates each of these windows.

\medskip
\noindent The full-horizon evidence the referee's single figure would have carried appears instead in Panel F of Table~1, which reports cumulative returns over the full episode, May 26, 1933 through February 18, 1935, in the same format as the event windows. The panel makes a point that supports our identification. Over these twenty-one months, both portfolios more than doubled, yet the cumulative differential between them is relatively small ($+8.4$ percentage points for the pooled sample). Exposed firms did not simply outperform non-exposed firms throughout the period; they outperformed around the specific dates at which news about the gold clauses arrived.
\medskip
\noindent One arithmetic feature of the table deserves a remark so that it does not read as an inconsistency: the pooled Joint Resolution differential ($+5.5$) exceeds each within-tercile differential ($+4.6$, $+4.9$, $+2.4$). This is a composition effect. The terciles are formed on common end-1932 market-capitalization breakpoints, and exposed firms---larger by assets but more levered, hence smaller by equity value---fall disproportionately in the small tercile (39\% of exposed firms, against 31\% of non-exposed firms), which earned the largest returns in the window. A footnote in Section~3 makes this point.

\medskip
\noindent The muted response of larger firms is consistent with the smaller effect of gold exposure we document for large firms in the investment evidence (Internet Appendix Table~IA.18). The aggregation discussion in Section~5.6 now echoes this connection:

\begin{quote}
Allowing for size heterogeneity yields similar results in 1933 but a somewhat smaller aggregate effect in 1934, reflecting the concentration of capital among large firms that experienced more moderate gold clause effects, in line with the stock return evidence, where the event-window differentials were most visible among small firms (Table~1).
\end{quote}

\medskip
\noindent The referee also suggests presenting the return analysis in a regression framework. The return evidence is part of the descriptive historical narrative of Section~3, and we did not want to overwhelm the reader with formal analysis at that point of the paper; we therefore introduced as little formalism as possible while still carrying the point. Table~1 implements the comparison such a regression would run: the raw differential is numerically the coefficient on a gold-exposure indicator in a cross-sectional regression of event-window returns, and the beta-adjusted differential is its market-model counterpart.
