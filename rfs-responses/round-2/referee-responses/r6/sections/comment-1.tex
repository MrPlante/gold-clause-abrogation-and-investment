\bigskip

\noindent The referee raises a valid point, and we now address it explicitly in the manuscript. Preferred dividends are discretionary in a way that bond coupons are not---deferring them does not trigger default---and we agree that this distinction matters in distress. A footnote in Section~5.2 now states this caveat where the preferred-share placebo is introduced:

\begin{quote}
``One caveat of this placebo test is that preferred shares may also proxy for financial flexibility, which would also be consistent with the result. We discuss this limitation further in Section~5.5.''
\end{quote}

\medskip
\noindent Section~5.5 also closes with an explicit limitations paragraph; it reads in part:

\begin{quote}
``[B]ecause virtually all corporate bonds of the era carried gold clauses while preferred shares carried none, $\tilde{d}$ is entangled with the composition of a firm's fixed claims: preferred shares enter its denominator but not its numerator, so preferred-heavy firms have mechanically lower measured exposure. Because preferred dividends are discretionary and non-interest-bearing (although the flexibility was partial, as preferred issues of the era were typically cumulative), such firms may also have more flexibility in distress, raising the concern that $\tilde{d}$ partly captures a general sensitivity to downturns among bond-reliant firms \ldots\ An ideal design would vary gold-clause exposure while holding the debt-versus-preferred composition fixed; the institutional structure of the period precludes this.''
\end{quote}
